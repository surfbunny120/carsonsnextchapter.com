Portland, Oregon

Our primary community: what life looks like now through 2080.

Portland is the anchor. It's where daily life happens — careers, community, culture, restaurants, friends. The city isn't a compromise or a fallback; it's a genuinely good place to live that also happens to score well on climate resilience. The point is to build a rich life here while quietly preparing for the ways that life will shift over the coming decades.

Why Portland Specifically

Within the PNW, Portland offers the best combination of urban amenities, cost of living (relative to Seattle), cultural progressivism, food scene, and climate positioning. The Willamette Valley location gives it rain-fed water security, distance from the coast (tsunami), and enough urban mass to maintain services and infrastructure investment even under stress.

Climate Timeline

Now – 2035

Essentially today's Portland with slightly warmer summers. Smoke season continues developing as an annual rhythm. Housing market healthy. Good time to buy and retrofit.

2035 – 2050

Noticeable shift. Average of 23 days/year above 91°F (vs. ~13 today). Heat dome events every 8–10 years. Smoke seasons more consistent. Summers feel more like current Sacramento climate. Winters remain mild and wet. Growing season extends — home gardens thrive.

2050 – 2070

Portland's summer climate resembles current-day Medford/Ashland. Heat pumps become essential rather than optional. Some summer drought stress on landscaping. But: still green, still rainy October–May, still functional. Migration pressure from California and Southwest increases housing demand.

2070 – 2080s

Heat domes every 6 years. Smoke weeks are a fixture. But Portland remains one of the most livable major cities in the western US by a wide margin. Water supply intact. Infrastructure adapted. Community strong.

Best Neighborhoods

For climate resilience specifically, Portland's west side and inner southeast score best — they're higher elevation (better air drainage during inversions), closer to Forest Park's cooling effect, and away from industrial heat islands.

West Hills / SW Portland

Elevated, tree-covered, naturally cooler in heat events. Excellent schools. Walkable to downtown. Higher price point ($800K–1.5M) but strong long-term value. Check seismic soil maps — some slopes are landslide-prone.

Eastmoreland / Woodstock

Established, tree-lined, excellent bone structure. Reed College neighborhood brings culture. More affordable ($600K–900K). Flat terrain means simpler seismic retrofitting. Good community infrastructure.

Lake Oswego

Suburban but polished. Excellent services, good restaurants emerging. The lake provides local cooling. Strong property values. More car-dependent. $700K–1.2M for nice homes.

Alberta / Beaumont-Wilshire

NE Portland's cultural heart. Walkable, vibrant, progressive. Slightly more urban-heat-island exposed. Great food scene. $550K–850K. Younger energy, more diverse.

The Livability Case

Beyond resilience, Portland is simply a great city for the kind of life we want to live. The food scene is nationally recognized and wildly affordable compared to SF or Seattle. The cultural calendar is rich (symphony, theater, independent cinema, world-class bookstores). The outdoor access is extraordinary — Mt. Hood skiing is 90 minutes away, the coast is 90 minutes west, the Gorge is 45 minutes east. The political culture aligns with our values. And unlike many "climate haven" cities being discussed nationally (Duluth, Buffalo), Portland already has the infrastructure, economy, and cultural depth to support a fulfilling urban life.
